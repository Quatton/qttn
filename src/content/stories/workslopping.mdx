## The 5th Industrial Revolution

I can't deny that LLMs have improved my productivity tremendously.

Not only do they help me write code, but they have also made me willing to put effort into things I might otherwise have not:

- Cross-referencing between different sources of information
- Running ablation tests to trim down bloats
- Writing a newsletter (jk lol)

---

And I'm sure it's not just me.

That's why I've never been strongly against AI. Please do use it to your advantage.

I'm happy to see people without technical backgrounds using LLMs to create new software products.

Non artistic people can now create _art[^debatably]_ and make their technical work more presentable.

[^debatably]: some unkind people might disagree

---

So... what went wrong?

How come we still associate LLMs with low-quality slop?

But being able to say **"I don't know"** is a good start.

---

Some evidence suggests that people are reluctant to say "I don't know," even when it matters to be right[^1]

It's embarrassing to admit, "Oh, that was an oversight on my part. I haven't gotten around to that yet." So people may instead go with:

> I'd rather blindly trust AI's advice than admit that I don't know.

[^1]: AI advice suppresses people's willingness to say "I don't know", even when the advice is wrong and accuracy is incentivized (https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.13562)

---

This can be especially hard in high-context environments, where people may hint that you should figure out the answer yourself instead of asking directly.

You worry someone will tell you:

> That's your job to figure out.
>
> Just Google it. Ask ChatGPT.

---

Humbling, I know, but hear me out: if someone actually tells you that, it says more about them than it does about you.

The fact that you asked means you were seeking their judgment on the issue.

You would not ask from a nobody on an obscure forum nor an LLM that might not have the context of your situation.

As long as you made sure you did your own research and asked a well-formed question, you did your part.

---

### AI doesn't make people lazy

It makes being lazy more _actionable_.

You used to be able to let lazy students go about their day without doing their homework.

Now they've learned to fuсk around more and still never find out.

---

The problem isn't that AI _always_ produces low-quality work.

In fact, the problem is that it can produce _polished_ work that you can hardly tell whether human judgment was applied at all.

---

Copy-and-paste an entire Wikipedia article?

Google Translate your homework from your native language in an English class?

Take images from copyrighted sources and slap them on your work without attribution?

### These have been around for decades.

---

We had a hard time explaining it because saying

> It strips away the human judgment and defeats the purpose of learning and evaluation

<Image src={EmojiNerd} alt="🤓" class="w-3/5 max-w-2xl" />

...is nerd as _sнit_.

---

In other words, it takes a high level of self-awareness to examine your own actions in a wider system.

Don't get me wrong: this has nothing to do with intelligence or anyone's worth as a person. In fact, I'd argue that the lack of ability to do so is part of what makes us human.

Not often do you stop to think about how your actions affect the world around you.

---

We relied on the fact that copied work was often visibly "low-quality" and easy to distinguish from work shaped by effort and judgment.

Our teachers used to be able to say, "Don't cite from Wikipedia. It's not a reliable source," only because

### It was actually unreliable at the time

---

Now it's not. Wikipedia is now relatively reliable in many areas, thanks to contributors who work constantly to improve it.

So "double-check your sources" can sound like "it might be wrong," and that warning starts to lose its weight.

---

Well, it might still be wrong, but more importantly, you never stop to think:

> What if there's a better explanation?
>
> Could I find another source that addresses the counterargument?
>
> Why has this article been edited so many times?

You call it done when there were still many unknowns left to explore.

---

The same goes for AI.

If our only objection to AI is that its output looks like "slop," that objection weakens as the output improves.

You might even say we should be _grateful_ that AI still produces sloppy work distinguishable from real human effort.

---

This article is written by me. AI could probably write it faster, better, and more coherently. So why bother writing it myself?

Of course, because I want this article to reflect my own perspective.

Word choice, phrasing, even carefully chosen memes all contribute to conveying the message behind my work.

---

### Once it doesn't matter anymore who did the work, it starts to matter more who did the work.

It might sound like one of those shallow word-swapped quotes.

However, the point is that "who did the work"-credibility will soon be replaced by "who did the work"-accountability.

---

And once that happens, well, genuinely,

## I don't care

---

Caring, acting, and taking responsibility are three different things.

AI lowers the barrier to actions that can look like they care, but they can't take responsibility on our behalf.

---

### Is vibe-coding inherently bad?

So-called "vibe-coders" can now produce a _polished_ prototype that works in a few hours.

However, they didn't take the time to understand the basic principles of the system they built. So they are being called out for their lack of _care and responsibility_ in the work they produced.

---

### But does it really matter?

Perhaps it does matter for them. Though I believe we're putting our anger in the wrong place.

If that tool stays a prototype, remains an internal admin-only dashboard, and never affects you, it's kinda absurd to get upset about it.

What do you think you are actually mad about?

---

It's when this work crosses

### 💥 The Interpersonal Boundary 💥

---

There are two failure modes here:

1. It becomes easier to perform concern without taking responsibility. Once you pull the "**Claude said:**" card, you distance yourself from words and actions you should have owned.

2. People who genuinely care are forced to become more skeptical. When something goes wrong, the response becomes: "You knew it was AI-generated. **You should have doubted it.**" The burden shifts to the person who failed to catch their coworker's mistake.

This commonly applies to heavily pipelined agentic workflows with a giant asterisk:

> AI might make mistakes, hopefully not too often, but good luck ❤️

Reminded you of anything?

---

Years ago when self-driving cars were new, there was a debate about how much responsibility should lie with the driver versus the system.

It was clear from the start. It was never about the possibility of mistakes, nor how perfect the system could be.

The very existence of the debate itself already proves the lack of attribution framework. The system builder can keep arguing in good faith, and shift the responsibility to the regulators.

---

> Hey, if it's not good I will just put more effort into making it better. Otherwise, just make a law to stop me.

> You're stopping me? Why are you against the development of the thing that will help the humanity?

After some back and forth, now they get more funding to improve the system and at the same time have new regulations that prevent new players in the space.

---

Good thing we still have some sensible policies in place.

[Some emerging repositories](https://github.com/cloudflare/cloudflare-os#contributing) are now explicitly disallowing outside contributions.

> AI has made writing code easy. The hard part, today, is not writing the code, but reviewing it, making sure quality stays high, and keeping the product coherent.
>
> — Cloudflare OS maintainers

---

### But I actually care! I put effort into verifying my work!

Good for you. In fact, I'd say

> Everybody will have to verify their own work before submission.

is a sensible rule. But it only works when **everybody** actually follows it.

---

Ironically, the rule becomes an unspoken rule once it is explicitly spoken.

You stop asking, "Did you actually verify your work?" because doubting that someone followed the agreed-upon rule can seem offensive. It's less effective if

> ✅ I verified my work

means nothing and you have to double-check it anyway.

---

But do you think when someone didn't follow the rule, does it mean they were malicious?

---

I like to think that being intentionally malicious at work is not an effective strategy at all. I don't think anyone is inherently malicious.

Most of your peers aren't acting with bad intentions. They have more to lose than to gain; they're just trying to get by and do their work.

This kind of behavior can emerge naturally in a system that punishes people for being efficient and for caring about their work. It's a hawk-and-dove game theory all over again.

---

## Now that we have AI...

> Isn't it cheap to just ask Claude to prototype this? I wanna see if rewriting an entire codebase with AI, reverse-engineering a well-known product from scratch, will improve our metric B and help us sell more to customers.
>
> Right?

---

If you want an answer to that, [Things you should never do, Part 1 (2000)](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/) is even **OLDER** than me. And yet, it is still relevant today.

---

[The Decoherence Trap](https://maevyn.codes/decoherence) spells out the same idea much more clearly than I'm about to. I highly recommend reading it.

We live in a world that rewards people for being locally coherent, but ignores the systemic incoherence that emerges from the number of interactions between its parts.

---

> I'm gonna pretend I know what the system is doing here.

Those unfilled gaps will become future sink-holes.

> I'm gonna pretend I care and that my actions are meaningful.

And we are left with a system that cares about an intermittent metric, but folds under the weight of its own complexity.

> I'm gonna pretend the cost we can measure is the only cost there is.

We ship sooner, celebrate small wins, and shift the burden to the next person maintaining the system.

## `workslopping`

<br className="mb-4" />

**_\[n.\]_** The act of cognitively offloading work to another human being regardless of efficiency, effort, or intent, often seen in complex, high-pressure environments, and commonly (but mistakenly) rewarded by the localization of the system.

---

## What about 'me' me? What about today?

Until here I have been mostly talking from an observer's perspective, from a hypothetical timeframe. There is no strict use of first person pronoun (I have been using third-person 'I' artistically) nor a clear sense of present.

Yes, I know that it isn't easy to stay fully aware when you're a part of the system itself.

---

I acknowledge that it takes a lot of social and emotional courage to push back on changes, while also communicating how it does not mean you are entirely against the idea.

---

There are deadlines. There are contract obligations and debts chained from one company to another company down the line. They need your work to do their work that someone else needs.

You can't really say "but wait" without paying an opportunity cost.

---

Breaking the fourth wall is tricky and can make your idea seem less practical. You risk losing credibility by being hypocritical.

> Aw, but I use AI to write code too so I don't have a say in this. Let's just each turn a blind eye on one another.

---

Speaking of breaking the fourth wall, I admit that writing this article is really awkward. I started writing this article a month ago and had been shaping it slowly since then to make it coherent.

---

I like to think that it's okay, and this awkwardness strengthens the argument itself.

It's an evidence that being aware does not make it easier to avoid.

---

### Why don't I become the decision maker myself?

Exactly, because I know it's hard and I did literally explain it earlier.

I got asked a lot why I chose to be employed instead of freelancing or starting my own business, especially given my views on workslopping.

It's much harder to notice circular involution when you're working alone.

---

The first week of my job was a breeze. This project? I could have done the entire thing in a day. I had dreams of many features of our product already planned, and I built PoC relatively quickly.

---

But the reality hit me soon after when I added more layers on top of things. The only way to validate my ideas was to see them folding against itself, exceeding my cognitive limits, even before it is used by anyone.

---

There was no security concerns, bugs, or performance issues yet. Zero customers; nobody is dissatisfied. It's purely internal chaos.

Again, I can't stress this enough that slop was never about low quality but rather the cognitive load it expects to decipher.

---

Things have changed and I learned to push back requirements, admit that it doesn't make any sense, and be willing to correct my mistakes. Even the correction itself can be wrong and it's okay to convince them to push forward.

---

The perk of being employed is my work can be viewed from a different angle and wider perspective. This means it is less punishing to be wrong. And boy do I love being wrong sometimes.

I get a free experiment space to try things without too much risk.

And it's never too late to go back and keep dreaming about new ideas when I learn how to properly manage your cognitive load.

---

I don't know what the point of being self-employed or building a startup is for others, and I absolutely respect it.

---

For me, if I have a problem with a target audience I care about, I'd prefer not to exploit it for profit.

And if I have a problem I care about without a target audience, I would rather not pursue or invent a problem for them.

Because even when I work alone, I also choose not to pass this `workslopping` onto my customers.

---

Until here you might notice that I did not treat `workslopping` as purely negative but rather natural part of work.

Unfortunately, it might happen to you and you might cause it onto others.

Please be mindful and try to manage it well.
