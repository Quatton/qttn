## I use AI a lot at work

One advantage of AI is that it makes tedious but valuable tasks more cost-effective. I would not put manual labor into these tasks otherwise.

- Cross-referencing between different sources of information
- Running ablation tests to trim down bloats from my code
- Pattern-matching what I did for one task onto another
- Writing a newsletter (jk lol)

I never _hate_[^1] AI and I'm happy to see people compensating for gaps in their background in one area and magnifying their capabilities in another.

If you don't know how to code, don't let people gatekeep you from building cool stuff.

## The Interpersonal Boundary

---

There are two failure modes here.

1.  > But this was supposed to be done by you...

This happens when the human judgement and the process of creating the work itself matter more than the final result.

Asking for someone's input and then having them go with "Claude said" can feel frustrating.

Art communities often value the process more than the final piece. Putting effort into esoteric details shows your target audience you care, and that's the message in and of itself.

---

Of course using a tool for shortcuts is not inherently bad.

There is even a level to this. For example, some purists might say "photography isn't art."

That does not matter if you share it to a photography club, so figure out who your audience is.

---

At the end of the day, remind yourself of [The Death of the Author](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_of_the_Author).

If your audience thinks 0.1\% that AI helped destroy the point you were making, then either change your target audience or respect them.

Stating your opinion based on an AI response or does not help. Your audience could have just asked the AI themselves.

---

2.  > This was definitely unconfirmed so I must check

Some work, like research, technical claims, or software code, needs careful verification, and the end product matters most.

Your audience (e.g., in this case, reviewers, auditors, or QA) will not necessarily care if it was automated; in fact, they might often hope that it was, because you are more prone to human error.

This is why Math, Computer Science, and Software Engineering welcome AI with open arms because we are always eager to abstract and automate our work as long as it can be trusted.

---

But if you blur the line between human and AI work and shift the burden to the person who failed to catch the mistake, this now becomes a problem of trust and accountability.

> Oopsie. Claude did it. Sorry, AI might make mistakes sometimes.

Does this remind you of anything?

---

Years ago, when self-driving cars were new, there was a debate about how much responsibility should lie with the driver versus the system.

It was clear from the start. It was never about the definition of "mistakes", nor how perfect the system could be.

---

Why are we even discussing who to hit if the very existence of the debate itself already proves the lack of an attribution framework?

---

[The Decoherence Trap](https://maevyn.codes/decoherence) spells out the same idea much more clearly than I'm about to. I highly recommend reading it.

We ship sooner, celebrate small wins, and shift the burden to the next person maintaining the system. And this leads to the third component of the `workslopping` problem.

We reward people for being locally coherent but ignores the **systemic incoherence** that emerges from the number of interactions between its parts.

## `workslopping`

<br className="mb-4" />

**_\[n.\]_** The act of cognitively offloading work to another human being regardless of efficiency, effort, or intent, often seen in complex, high-pressure environments, and commonly (but mistakenly) rewarded by the local optimization yet resulting in net-negative outcomes.

---

### But Do 'I' Make `workslop`?

Until here, we have been mostly discussing it from an observer's perspective, in a hypothetical timeframe.

There was no strict use of a first-person pronoun (I have been using third-person 'I' artistically) nor a clear sense of the present.

Now let's talk about 'I' as in me, from my own experience.

Yes, I know that it isn't easy to stay fully aware when you're a part of the system itself.

---

I acknowledge that it takes a lot of social and emotional courage to push back on changes, while also communicating how it does not mean you are entirely against the idea.

---

There are deadlines. There are contract obligations and debts chained from one coworker to another, from one company to another company down the line. They need your work to do their work that someone else needs.

You can't really say "but wait" without paying an opportunity cost.

---

Breaking the fourth wall to say this makes my idea less practical. I risk losing credibility by being hypocritical.

> Aw, but I use AI to write code too, so I don't have a say in this. Let's just each turn a blind eye on one another.

I admit that writing this article is really awkward. I started writing this article a month ago and have been shaping it slowly since then to make it coherent.

---

I like to think that it's okay, and this awkwardness strengthens the argument itself.

It's evidence that being aware does not make it easier to avoid.

### Why don't I become the decision maker myself?

I got asked a lot why I chose to be employed instead of freelancing or starting my own business, especially given my views on workslopping.

It's much harder to notice circular, side-way involution when you're working alone.

---

The first week of my job was a breeze. This project? I could have done the entire thing in a day. I had dreams of many features of our product already planned, and I built many small PoCs relatively quickly.

---

But the reality hit me soon after I added more layers on top of things. The only way to validate my ideas was to see them folding against themselves, exceeding my cognitive limits, even before it is used by anyone.

---

There were no security concerns, bugs, or performance issues yet. It was purely internal chaos. Zero customers; nobody was dissatisfied.

Again, I can't stress enough that slop was never about low quality but rather the burden it leaves you to decipher.

---

Things have changed, and I learned to push back on requirements, admit that it doesn't make any sense, yet also be willing to correct my mistakes.

Even the correction itself can be wrong, and it's okay to convince them that we can push forward.

---

The perk of being employed is that my work can be viewed from a different angle and a wider perspective.

This means it is less punishing to be wrong. And boy do I love being wrong sometimes.

---

Moreover, I get a low-risk space to experiment; they get to keep innovating. That's a win-win for me.

And it's never too late to go back and keep dreaming about new ideas when I learn how to properly manage my cognitive load.

---

To be honest, I don't know what the point of being self-employed or building a startup is for others, and I absolutely respect it.

For me, if I have a problem with a target audience I care about, I'd prefer not to exploit it for profit.

And if I have a problem I care about without a target audience, I would rather not pursue or invent a problem for them.

Because even when I work alone, I also choose not to pass this `workslopping` onto my customers.

---

Until here, you might notice that I did not treat `workslopping` as inherently negative but rather as a natural part of work that will soon result in negative outcomes.

Unfortunately, it will happen to you, and you might cause it to happen to others too.

Please be mindful and try to manage it well.
