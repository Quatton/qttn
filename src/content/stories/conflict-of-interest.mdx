# Conflict of Interest Is Worse Than Lack of Incentive

What if business skills aren't all it takes to run a successful company?

---

When I was in college (as if that was a long time ago), I dreamed of starting a company.

---

I had been a somewhat competent programmer, so I really believed that if I could just pick one little problem in my niche, find long-term customers, and build a product, I could make a living doing what I loved.

---

I wasn't wrong?

Because although now I technically cop out and joined a big-tech company, I still do what I love.
And, yeah I still make a living without worrying if my company will survive or not.

---

My parent company has a lot of money. We didn't overhire unlike 2022-era startups.

I don't have to expect that one day my badge won't scan, at least for the next 3-5 years. (Pessimistic? Maybe. I'd rather call it realistic.)

---

So I... should be happy, right? What went wrong? Why do I feel like I'm not doing what I love?

---

## Do we lack the skills, or do we lack the incentive to care?

---

I had a 1-on-1 with my manager a few weeks ago, and I asked him why our product team promises to deliver features that we didn't have the bandwidth to ship and why engineering team members are overworked yet still unmotivated to ship the features.

---

Yet if you ask the engineers what they want to work on, they will have a long list of things. Sure, it's always a beautiful architecture with a lot of interesting problems to solve but we hallucinate the needs half of the time. Those ideas ended up under the "nice-to-have" category.

---

My manager believed that the problem is that engineers **don't have enough expertise** in the business domain. He thought that if engineers understood the business process, product delivery process, and customer needs, they would be more motivated to ship features because they would understand the value of their work.

---

Sounds reasonable, right? And that was my first thought too.

---

Same day, during our coffee break, the engineers were talking about the same topic. Our quality has been declining, and we received quite a lot of complaints from a major customer. One engineer said,

> I'll be happy to step up and take ownership of the problem. I'll even ensure that there will be zero bugs and 100% user satisfaction

---

> Of course, if they pay me more...

---

Cold hard cash always wins cold hard truth. We all know that we stand in this exact place because we have to put food on the table.

Promotions? Nobody really cares about the additional title or responsibility. We are all aiming for the extra compensation.

---

So now we are putting bandaids onto a broken system. We motivate great talents to spend their skill points on a bigger problem.

> Easy, easy, I know you don't like this job but look! A hundred thousand dollars more in your bank account, what do you say?

---

Managers say engineers lack the skills.

Engineers say we'd care more if we were paid more.

Who's right?

---

## What if this is bound to happen?

---

It's 2026, skills are more accessible than ever before (to the point where we anthropomorphize skills as SKILL.md files in our GitHub repos.) If you **actually** need to do something at your job, it's not "ah sorry boss, I can't" but rather that you go figure out how to do it.

---

And, your salary has always been performance-based and reflects your contribution to the company. So theoretically, you should be able put infinite effort into your work and get infinite compensation.

So, go ahead, do it!

---

Wait... but why should we even work **for a company** in the first place? Why not just work **for ourselves**?

Learn infinite skills, build infinite products, target 8 billion people, and make infinite money.

---

Let's not get ahead of ourselves by strawmanning the argument. I'll show you a smaller, realistic example.

---

Say, let's pick up a product from my graveyard. I used to work on a product called "Memoiz", which originally started as a mood tracker with a diary you can ask questions to. It was simple. The value proposition was really clear. I am my own first customer and I was really motivated to ship the product.

---

When the context shifted from a week-long hackathon, I had to actually formalize the product and make it a real business. We were just two college students navigating the world of business.

---

Now this exact moment qualified the first point: we were beyond the argument of "do we have the business skills to ship a product?" but rather "now it's time to have the business skills to ship a product."

---

We ran a quick market research by interviewing potential customers. We also did look into business use cases (of a mood tracker? eh who knows, Modern Health is a genuine mental health product for enterprises.)

---

We also found many competitors in the market. From the core "semantic search as a service" to the "mood tracker with a diary", meaning that we sliced up our product vertically and horizontally to find a niche that we could dominate.

---

And, yeah, built a landing page with a product demo that meets all the rules of a good landing page.

---

So what went wrong? Why did we fail to ship the product?

---

It's... because we tried to do everything.

Not about the vertical slicing our product or whatever that was. But rather, we were compensating for the lack of experience with more research and more work.

---

Are we not paid enough?

Money was never the problem. We weren't paid to begin with and look at how much work we did. We were highly motivated to ship something valuable to our customers (even if they were non-existent at the time.)

---

I reflected back to the time when I was implementing a new feature based on the research we did.
